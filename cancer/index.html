A guided journey

Understanding stage 4 pancreatic cancer

What it means, how it's treated, how to stay as comfortable as possible, and where to find help — one step at a time.

Begin the journey ↓
This is general education, not medical advice. Every person is different — please talk with your oncology team about your own care.
Chapter 01

What's happening inside

i.

The body's quiet workshop

Tucked deep behind the stomach, the pancreas makes enzymes that digest food and hormones, like insulin, that control blood sugar.

ii.

The pancreas

About 90% of pancreatic cancers are pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), which starts in the cells lining the small ducts. Because the organ is so deep, early tumors rarely cause symptoms.

iii.

A tumor forms

Cancer cells grow out of control and form a tumor — most often in the head of the pancreas, near the bile duct. That's why jaundice is a common first sign.

iv.

It spreads to the liver

Cancer cells can travel through the blood. The liver is the most common place pancreatic cancer spreads to.

v.

This is stage 4

Stage 4 (metastatic) means the cancer has reached distant organs — the liver, the lining of the abdomen (peritoneum), the lungs, or sometimes the bones.

About half of people are diagnosed at this stage. It's generally not curable — but it is treatable, and treatment can help people live longer and feel better.

Chapter 02

The signs it sends

Symptoms come from the tumor itself and from wherever it has spread. Most people have several at once.

◐

Pain

Upper belly pain that may wrap around to the back — often worse after eating or when lying flat.

◑

Jaundice

Yellow skin or eyes, dark urine, pale stools and itching, when the tumor blocks the bile duct.

◒

Weight loss & fatigue

Unplanned loss of weight and muscle (cachexia), low appetite and deep tiredness.

◓

Digestive changes

Oily, floating, foul-smelling stools, bloating, nausea, or feeling full quickly.

◔

New diabetes

Diabetes that appears suddenly — especially after 50 — or becomes hard to control.

◕

Blood clots

A swollen, painful leg or sudden shortness of breath. Get medical help right away.

Chapter 03

Finding answers

Imaging

CT scan

A pancreas-protocol CT is the main test. MRI, PET or endoscopic ultrasound may add detail.

Tissue

Biopsy

A sample — often from a liver spot or by endoscopic ultrasound — confirms the diagnosis and is used for molecular testing.

Blood

CA 19-9

A tumor marker that helps track how treatment is working. It isn't used alone to diagnose, and some people don't make it.

Don't skip these

Two tests every patient should ask about

1 · Germline (inherited) genetic testing

Recommended for everyone with pancreatic cancer. Changes in genes like BRCA1, BRCA2 or PALB2 can open up specific treatments — and matter for family members.

2 · Tumor biomarker (molecular) testing

Looks for changes in the cancer itself — KRAS status, NTRK, RET or NRG1 fusions, BRAF, and MSI-high/dMMR. A small but important share of patients have a targetable change.

Chapter 05

Care that surrounds you

Palliative care focuses on comfort and quality of life. It's not hospice — you can get it alongside chemotherapy, starting at diagnosis. In advanced cancers, early palliative care improves quality of life and mood.

You, at the center

Pain control

Medicines, and a celiac plexus block that can greatly ease belly and back pain.

Digestion

Pancreatic enzymes (PERT) with meals help absorb food and keep weight on.

Blockages

A stent can open a blocked bile duct or stomach outlet.

Nutrition

A dietitian helps with small, frequent, high-calorie meals and taste changes.

Blood clots

Clots are common; a blood thinner may be recommended.

Mind & heart

Anxiety and depression are common and treatable. Counselors and groups help.

Call your care team right away for fever during chemo, new or worsening jaundice, vomiting you can't stop, sudden shortness of breath, a swollen leg, black stools or vomiting blood, or pain that isn't controlled.
Chapter 06

Looking at the numbers

It's okay to want — or not want — to see statistics. They describe large groups of people from past years and can't predict what will happen to any one person.

"Median" means half of people lived longer than this — some much longer. Outcomes are slowly improving, and people with certain biomarkers, strong responses, or access to newer therapies can do better than these averages.

Behind every number is a person — and every person's story is their own.
